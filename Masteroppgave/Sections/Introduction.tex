\section{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

Artificial intelligence has undergone a dramatic transformation over the past decade, emerging as one of the most powerful technological forces of our time. Recent advances in deep learning and large-scale optimization have produced systems capable of performing tasks once considered exclusive to human intelligence. Transformer-based language models can generate coherent text, write code, and assist in complex reasoning tasks \parencite{vaswani2017attention,brown2020language}. Reinforcement learning systems have achieved superhuman performance in games such as Go \parencite{silver2016mastering}, while breakthroughs in scientific machine learning have enabled major progress on long-standing challenges such as protein structure prediction \parencite{jumper2021highly}. Taken together, these developments suggest that artificial intelligence is not only progressing rapidly, but may be approaching a level of capability that raises fundamental questions about the feasibility of artificial general intelligence.

Despite this progress, it is highly debated whether anything resembling artificial general intelligence can be reached simply by scaling today’s methods. Modern AI systems are increasingly powerful, but they still differ from biological intelligence in several important ways. In particular, they continue to face challenges that suggest that more data, larger models, and more compute may not be the full answer. Among the most important are:
\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Energy intensity and consumption.} Modern AI systems require enormous amounts of computation and energy. Until around 2012, compute demand for major AI models roughly followed Moore's law, doubling approximately every 24 months. Since then, it has accelerated sharply, with recent frontier models doubling compute demand as quickly as every 2 months \parencite{mehonic2021brain}. This energy problem becomes especially clear at biological scale: a digital simulation of an artificial neural network approximately the size of the human brain would consume \(7.9\,\mathrm{MW}\). This illustrates how energy-intensive conventional digital approaches can become, and suggests that simply scaling current systems may face serious efficiency limits.

    \item \textbf{Lack of continual learning.} An intelligent system should be able to learn from new experiences and information over time. For example, an AI assistant should be able to adapt to a user's preferences, and a model deployed in the real world should be able to incorporate important new events after its initial training. In current AI systems, however, updating a model after deployment is often difficult because new learning can interfere with old knowledge, a problem known as catastrophic forgetting \parencite{mccloskey1989catastrophic}. As a result, many current systems rely on a separate training phase followed by a deployment phase, where the model's knowledge is largely fixed.

    \item \textbf{Limited generalization and abstraction.} Current AI systems often perform well on tasks that resemble their training data, but become less reliable in novel situations. One reason is that models may rely on statistical regularities or superficial correlations rather than learning abstract concepts that transfer. For example, a model trained to recognize chairs may rely on common features such as four legs, a backrest, or a familiar shape. If it is then shown an unusual chair, such as an artistic design with one thick central support or a shape unlike the examples seen during training, it may fail to recognize it as a chair, even though its function as something to sit on is clear. Robust intelligence requires concepts that generalize beyond the most common patterns in the training data \parencite{dehaene2021how}.
    
    \item \textbf{Lack of grounded causal understanding.} A final, more fundamental challenge is that many current AI systems learn primarily from static data rather than through interaction with the world. This can produce powerful statistical representations, but it is not the same as learning through action and consequence. For example, a language model may learn the word ``heavy'' from thousands of textual contexts, but this is different from learning heaviness by lifting an object and experiencing how weight affects movement. The concern is that systems trained mainly on passive data may learn correlations between concepts without grounding those concepts in causal experience. This is closely related to the argument that more general intelligence may require world models that are learned through interaction and used to predict the consequences of actions \parencite{lecun2022path}.

\end{itemize}

Biological systems provide a proof of existence that these challenges can be handled in practice. The energy contrast is especially striking: while a digital simulation of an artificial neural network approximately the size of the human brain would consume \(7.9\,\mathrm{MW}\), the human brain operates at roughly \(20\,\mathrm{W}\), corresponding to a difference of about six orders of magnitude \parencite{mehonic2021brain}. Biological systems also learn continuously. Humans do not have a separate training phase followed by a fixed deployment phase; we learn throughout life, incorporate new experiences, and usually do so without overwriting existing knowledge in the catastrophic way observed in artificial neural networks. Similarly, humans often form abstract concepts rather than only memorizing superficial regularities. A person can recognize an unusual chair as a chair, even if it differs from most chairs seen before, because the concept is tied to its function as something to sit on rather than only to features such as four legs or a familiar shape \parencite{dehaene2021how}. Finally, biological understanding is grounded in interaction with the world. We do not learn concepts such as heaviness only from descriptions, but by acting, sensing consequences, and building causal expectations from experience. These properties make biological intelligence a natural reference point when searching for more efficient, adaptive, and general learning systems.

Since biological systems handle many of the challenges above, it is natural to ask whether future AI systems should take more direct inspiration from biological neural architectures. The point is not that the brain can be copied directly today, but that biology may contain computational principles that current digital systems do not exploit well. This has motivated work on spiking neural networks, recurrent dynamics, neuromorphic hardware, and biological substrates. A recent example is Cortical Labs' CL1 system, which integrates living neurons with silicon hardware and allows biological neural networks to interact with software in real time. Building on earlier work where cultured neurons learned to play Pong \parencite{kagan2022vitro}, Cortical Labs has also demonstrated CL1 interacting with the game \emph{Doom} \parencite{corticallabs2026doom}. Such systems are still at a very early stage, but they illustrate that biological computation is no longer only a theoretical idea.

However, more biologically inspired architectures also require learning rules that are compatible with them. The dominant learning algorithm in modern AI is backpropagation, which trains virtually all current neural networks by computing exact gradients through a backward pass. This is extremely effective in artificial neural networks, but the mechanisms it relies on break sharply with biological learning: neurons and synapses do not appear to have access to precise neuron-specific error derivatives, a separate recursive backward computation, or feedback pathways that use exact copies of downstream weights in reverse \parencite{crick1989recent,lillicrap2020backpropagation}. This creates a central problem. If more biologically inspired architectures are ever to become useful learning systems, they cannot only borrow biological structure; they also need learning rules that can plausibly operate within that structure. In such settings, biologically plausible learning algorithms, which are the focus of this thesis, are not just an interesting alternative to backpropagation, but a necessary part of the broader search for more brain-like artificial intelligence. To provide a controlled setting for analysing such methods, this thesis tests them in standard artificial neural networks and compares them against backpropagation. However, because the methods are biologically plausible, they are also candidates for implementation in more biologically inspired architectures.

Several biologically Rplausible alternatives to backpropagation have been proposed, including feedback alignment, predictive coding, and the forward-forward algorithm \parencite{lillicrap2016random,nokland2016direct,rao1999predictive,whittington2017approximation,hinton2022forward}. This thesis focuses on perturbation-based learning, a conceptually simple family of methods where random noise is injected into the weights or neural activity and then correlated with a scalar performance signal. If the perturbation improves performance, it is reinforced; if it worsens performance, it is suppressed. This idea has been studied as a way to train neural networks since at least the early 1990s, and several variants have been proposed since then \parencite{widrow1990perceptrons,flower1992summed,williams1992simple,werfel2005learning}. These methods differ in where the noise is injected, how the performance signal is formed, and how the resulting update is assigned to weights.

Although perturbation-based learning is therefore an established research direction, several central questions remain unresolved. Recent work is largely organized around two main families: weight perturbation, where noise is injected into the parameters, and node perturbation, where noise is injected into neural activity. However, the relationship between these two families is still not fully understood. In addition, the practical capability of perturbation methods remains unclear: existing papers show that they can train neural networks, but do not provide a systematic picture of what types of tasks they can solve well, where they become inefficient, and where they begin to break down. Finally, despite the long history of perturbation-based learning, the literature is still relatively small compared with other alternatives to backpropagation. This leaves room both for a clearer theoretical comparison of existing methods and for the development of new perturbation-based learning rules.

These gaps make perturbation-based learning a natural focus for this thesis. The field is established enough that there is a meaningful literature to build on, but open enough that important theoretical and empirical questions remain unresolved. This motivates the three research questions and objectives stated below.
\subsection{Research Questions and Objectives}
The objective of this thesis is to investigate perturbation-based learning methods as biologically plausible alternatives to backpropagation. The thesis combines literature analysis, theoretical development, and empirical evaluation.

\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{What is the current state of perturbation-based learning?}  
    This question is addressed by reviewing and comparing existing perturbation-based learning methods, with emphasis on how different approaches inject noise, form learning signals, and update weights. The objective is to establish what is already understood, identify which existing variants are most relevant, and clarify where the literature remains incomplete.

    \item \textbf{How can we improve the current state ofperturbation-based learning?}  
    This question is addressed by building on the existing literature to clarify the relationship between common perturbation algorithms, and by using this understanding to develop a theoretically motivated variant of node perturbation. The objective is to further develop perturbation-based learning by strengthening its theoretical basis and identifying a modification that may improve estimator quality and learning performance.

    \item \textbf{What is the practical capability of perturbation-based learning compared with backpropagation?}  
    This question is addressed by evaluating existing perturbation-based learning methods and the proposed method across supervised learning tasks of increasing complexity. The objective is to understand both when these methods work well and when they begin to break down, using backpropagation as the standard reference method.

\end{itemize}
